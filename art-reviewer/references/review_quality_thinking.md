# Review Quality Thinking Framework

A cognitive framework for producing high-quality art-paper reviews. Teaches **how to think** about an art paper's quality, not just what to score. Grounded in the art-research evidence model (`shared/references/art_research_evidence_model.md`) — *the artwork is the primary evidence*.

## The Three Lenses

Every art paper should be evaluated through three lenses simultaneously:

### Lens 1: Anchoring — "Is each claim grounded in the work?"

Ask in order:
1. What is the central claim about the work (what it does, means, or achieves)?
2. What evidence is offered — the work as encountered, process/making, exhibition/reception, conceptual lineage, situated reflection (evidence model §2)?
3. Is there an anchor connecting the claim to that evidence? (Could a reader, in principle, verify it?)
4. Are there alternative readings of the work the artist didn't consider?
5. Would removing the single strongest piece of documentation collapse the case?

**If #5 is yes**: the case rests on one linchpin (often a single video or an unverifiable reception claim). Flag it — the contribution is only as strong as that one anchor.

### Lens 2: Concept — "Is there a load-bearing idea, stated as such?"

Ask in order:
1. Can you state the work's **concept** in one sentence (not its theme, not its technique — glossary §7)?
2. Does the work genuinely embody that concept, or is it asserted beside the work?
3. Does the concept stay stable across the paper, or drift in meaning?
4. Is the concept positioned against the right precedents (or claimed as novel without citing them)?
5. What are the boundary conditions — what does the work *not* claim?

**Judgment heuristic**: the most common art-paper failure is a paper that describes theme and technique but never states the concept. If you cannot articulate the concept, either it is absent or the paper has not communicated it — both are review-worthy.

### Lens 3: Contribution — "So what for the field?"

Ask in order:
1. What did the art-and-technology discourse have before this work?
2. What does it have after?
3. Is the delta meaningful (a genuine new step, not a familiar move restated)?
4. Who in the field benefits from encountering this work?
5. What new questions or directions does it open?

**Judgment heuristic**: if you can't articulate the delta in one sentence, either the contribution is weak or the paper hasn't communicated it. Both are review-worthy observations.

## Common Reviewer Traps

| Trap | Description | How to Avoid |
|------|-------------|-------------|
| **Statistical-rigor projection** | Demanding samples, power, p-values from a practice-based work | Use the art-research evidence model; anchoring, not statistics, is the criterion |
| **IMRaD tunnel vision** | Expecting Method/Results structure | Evaluate the chosen art-paper pattern on its own terms |
| **Taste projection** | Penalizing a work whose provocation is not to your taste | Evaluate how well it makes its case, not whether you endorse it |
| **Reception credulity** | Accepting "audiences were moved" at face value | Require an observable anchor; "moved/amazed" without one is reception inflation |
| **Novelty credulity** | Accepting "the first work to…" at face value | Require precedence claims to be citable; reward precise positioning |
| **Missing forest for trees** | Listing 20 minor issues, missing the one fatal flaw (e.g., no concept) | Always state the single most important issue first |

## Calibration Questions (ask after drafting your review)

1. If this work were presented as-is, would the paper mislead readers about what the work does or contributes? (If yes → Major Revision or Reject)
2. Could the artist reasonably address my concerns in one revision cycle? (If no → Reject)
3. Am I being harder on this work than I would be on my own practice? (Calibration check)
4. Did I identify at least one genuine strength? (Balance check)
5. Did I reward honest specificity rather than punish it, and flag over-claimed generalizability? (Evidence-model check)
6. Would my review help the artist strengthen the work and paper, even if rejected? (Constructiveness check)
