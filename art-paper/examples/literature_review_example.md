## Abstract

Generative and interactive installation increasingly composes itself from the audience's body, and a distinct strand keys to *involuntary* physiology — heartbeat, breath, gaze, skin conductance — rather than deliberate gesture. ## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 Topic and Rationale

The audience's body has become a recurring material in interactive installation. A growing body of work composes the artwork not from what the visitor deliberately does but from what their body does on its own — pulse, breath, gaze, galvanic response [Schiphorst 2021]. These works pose an artistic and ethical question distinct from gesture-driven interaction: when the work senses something you do not consciously control, what does authorship and consent mean?

The stakes extend beyond any single piece. As biosensing becomes cheaper and more pervasive, the conventions artists establish for sensing the involuntary body — how they disclose it, how they aggregate it, how they frame agency — shape both the art form and the wider culture of bodily data. Understanding how the field has handled the involuntary body is therefore valuable for both practice and criticism.

### 1.2 Scope and Boundaries

This review covers sources published between 2005 and 2025, focusing on:
- **Source types**: artworks and exhibitions (primary), artist writings and interviews, critical and theoretical texts
- **Domain**: generative and interactive installation that senses involuntary or semi-voluntary physiology
- **Excluded**: purely gesture- or position-driven interaction; clinical biofeedback outside an art context; screen-based generative art without an embodied sensing relation

Sources were located through art-and-technology proceedings and journals (e.g., *Leonardo*, media-art conference proceedings), exhibition catalogs and archives, and citation tracing from key works. From an initial pool of roughly 180 candidates, 52 met the inclusion criteria. ## 2. Theme 1: The Biosignal as Intimate-Yet-Anonymizable Material

### 2.1 Heartbeat as Foundational Material

Heartbeat has been the foundational involuntary material of biosignal installation. Lozano-Hemmer's *Pulse* series [Lozano-Hemmer 2007–2018] established a template: a single, intensely private signal — the visitor's heartbeat — captured at a station and then rendered at architectural scale, where it joins thousands of others and loses individual legibility. Critics have read this doubling, intimate at capture and anonymous in aggregate, as the series' central tension [Author F 2016]. The work demonstrates that an involuntary signal can be at once a portrait of one body and a portrait of none.

Subsequent station-based works extended heartbeat into sculptural and architectural registers [Author G 2018], but most retained the one-visitor-at-a-time capture model. This model makes the act of contributing legible and consensual — you step up, you place your hand — but it also frames the body as a discrete input rather than as continuous presence in a space.

### 2.2 Beyond Heartbeat: Gaze, Skin, and Breath

A second wave broadened the palette of involuntary signals. Eye-tracking installations made gaze a material [Author H 2019], surfacing the discomfort of being watched watching. Galvanic skin response has been used to expose arousal the visitor cannot mask [Author I 2020]. Breath, however, occupies a peculiar position that the literature has only begun to address: it is partly voluntary. Unlike heartbeat, a visitor can hold or pace their breath — so a breath-driven work sits on a hinge between the involuntary and the controlled [Author J 2022]. The review finds that this volitional ambiguity is frequently noted but rarely made the explicit subject of a work.

### 2.3 Summary of Theme 1

The biosignal-as-material literature establishes a productive tension between intimacy and anonymity, largely worked out through heartbeat at single-visitor stations. Breath, with its volitional ambiguity, and room-scale continuous sensing of many bodies at once, remain comparatively under-explored [Author J 2022].

## 3. Theme 2: Feedback Legibility versus Surrender

### 3.1 The Control-and-Feedback Paradigm

Much interactive-art theory frames audience agency in terms of legible feedback: the participant exercises agency by understanding how their action maps to the work's response, and good interaction design makes that mapping discoverable [Costello and Edmonds 2019]. Edmonds's taxonomy of interactive art [Edmonds 2018] and a broad HCI-influenced literature treat this discoverability as a design goal. Within this paradigm, an opaque mapping is a usability failure.

### 3.2 The Limits of Legibility for Involuntary Signals

A counter-current questions whether legibility is the right ideal for works keyed to the involuntary body. When the material is heartbeat or breath, tight, perceptible action-response coupling may actually break the experience — turning a contemplative encounter into a game of control [Author K 2021]. Schiphorst's somatic framing [Schiphorst 2021] proposes attention to first-person bodily experience rather than to mapping legibility, implicitly valuing a stance of attending and yielding over one of commanding. Bishop's broader critique of participation [Bishop 2012] cautions against equating audience activity with meaningful authorship at all.

These positions converge on an alternative model of agency that the present review names *surrender*: the visitor authors the work by inhabiting and yielding rather than by commanding, and legibility is deliberately withheld. The literature gestures toward this model but rarely names it or examines it through a specific exhibited work.

### 3.3 Slow and Aggregate Responses

Related to surrender is the use of slow and aggregate response. Generative-music lineages [Eno 1996] and slow gallery soundscapes refuse the instant feedback loop, responding on timescales of minutes. Aggregating many visitors into one room-level signal similarly dissolves individual legibility [Author L 2023]. Both moves are, in effect, design choices that push the experience away from control and toward surrender, though they are seldom theorized as such.

### 3.4 Summary of Theme 2

The dominant control-and-feedback paradigm fits gesture-driven work but is contested for involuntary-signal work, where a model of surrender — withheld legibility, slow and aggregate response — appears better suited. This model is gestured at across the literature but lacks a named framework grounded in a specific work.

## 4. Theme 3: Disclosure and Consent as Compositional Concerns

### 4.1 When the Audience Learns What Is Sensed

A recurring but under-developed thread concerns *disclosure*: when and how a work tells the audience what it is sensing. Station-based heartbeat works disclose by design — the act of contributing is explicit. Room-scale and ambient works, which sense visitors continuously and often without their initiating an act, raise the question of disclosure acutely [Author M 2024]. Some artists place explanatory text at the entrance; others withhold it until exit, making discovery part of the work. The review finds that disclosure timing is treated mostly as curatorial signage rather than as a compositional material.

### 4.2 Consent and the Involuntary Body

Sensing an involuntary, intimate act without explicit consent carries an ethical charge that the critical literature has flagged [Author N 2023] but that practitioner accounts rarely engage in depth. The discomfort of being unknowingly sensed — even when data is anonymized and discarded — is documented in criticism of biosignal and surveillance-adjacent art [Author N 2023], yet few first-person accounts describe how artists have handled this in an actual exhibition.

### 4.3 Summary of Theme 3

Disclosure and consent are recognized as ethically charged for involuntary-body work, but are under-theorized as artistic material and under-documented from the practitioner's situated perspective.

## 5. Cross-Cutting Synthesis

### 5.1 Convergent Observations

Across all three themes, two observations recur:

1. **Involuntary-signal work troubles the control paradigm.** Works keyed to the body's own activity resist the legibility ideal and point toward a model of agency as surrender (noted across Themes 2 and 3).
2. **Aggregation and slowness do double duty**, serving both as technical strategies and as artistic moves that secure anonymity and the surrender stance.

### 5.2 Divergent Positions and Debates

The literature contains unresolved tensions:
- **Legibility vs. opacity**: whether the audience should understand the mapping, or whether withholding it is the point.
- **Disclosure timing**: entrance disclosure (informed but un-surprising) vs. exit disclosure (surprising but potentially non-consensual).
- **Activity vs. authorship**: whether audience bodily contribution constitutes authorship at all [Bishop 2012].

### 5.3 Source-Base Observations

The reviewed sources are dominated by single-work criticism and artist statements; situated reception writing — accounts of how visitors actually behaved during an exhibition — is scarce. Heartbeat dominates the artwork base; breath and room-scale continuous sensing are comparatively rare.

## 6. Gaps and a Positioning of Practice

### 6.1 Identified Gaps

1. **Breath as distinct material**: heartbeat dominates; breath's volitional ambiguity is noted but rarely made the subject of a work.
2. **Room-scale continuous aggregate sensing**: most work senses one visitor at a time at a station.
3. **A named model of agency-as-surrender** grounded in a specific exhibited work.
4. **Situated practitioner reception accounts** of how visitors negotiated authorship.
5. **Disclosure timing as compositional material**, rather than as signage.

### 6.2 Where a Practice-Based Contribution Sits

A practice-based contribution that (a) uses breath, (b) senses the whole room continuously and aggregately, (c) withholds legibility to invite surrender, and (d) treats disclosure timing as a composed material, addressing the situated reception gap through first-person exhibition reflection, would sit precisely at the intersection of the three themes above.

## 7. Conclusion

### 7.1 Key Takeaways

1. Generative installation keyed to the involuntary body troubles the control-and-feedback paradigm of interactive-art discourse.
2. A model of agency-as-surrender, supported by aggregation and slowness, is gestured at but not named or grounded in a specific work.
3. Disclosure and consent are ethically charged and under-theorized as artistic material.
4. Situated reception writing from practitioners is scarce.

### 7.2 Implications for Practice and Criticism

Artists working with the involuntary body should treat disclosure timing as part of the composition and document situated reception in their own terms. Critics should develop a vocabulary for agency-as-surrender that does not import the control paradigm wholesale.
